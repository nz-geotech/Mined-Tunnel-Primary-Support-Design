Multiple-Graph Limitation

The GSI chart presented in Graph 1 is applied strictly within the limits defined by Hoek et al. (2013). Therefore, highly sheared rock masses that lack blockiness due to closely spaced shear planes and do not exhibit interlocking block behavior have been excluded. Likewise, intact or massive rock with a GSI greater than 80 has also been omitted, since brittle failure mechanisms such as rockbursts and spalling occur without the rotation or translation of interlocking blocks. These failure processes do not conform to the assumed conditions of structural homogeneity inherent in the definition of GSI. To prevent misinterpretation, rock masses classified as intact or massive with GSI values above 80 have not been considered in the GSI chart shown in Graph 1.

Special supporting measures are required in zones affected by heavy squeezing, swelling, spalling, or rock bursting, as these conditions fall outside the scope of both the GSI framework and the NGI (2025) Q-system. In such areas, support design incorporates a combination of adaptable and flexible systems, which may include, but are not limited to, the following:

Heavy Squeezing Zone

Support measures should allow controlled deformation and stress relief through planned convergence. This may include over-excavation, the use of yielding (ductile) rock bolts, and shotcrete linings incorporating sliding joints in steel sets or lattice girders, deformable elements, shotcrete slots, or lining stress controllers (LSC). Pre-excavation ground improvement and pre-support measures such as spiling or pipe canopies are also recommended. Mechanical excavation techniques with reduced advance lengths (typically less than 1.0 m), using split-face excavation or a pilot tunnel approach, may be necessary to ensure stability and maintain construction safety.

Swelling Rocks

Certain rock types, such as shales or rocks containing swelling minerals like anhydrite, gypsum, or montmorillonite (especially within joint infill), can generate significant swelling pressures on tunnel support systems when exposed to groundwater or even moisture uptake from the atmosphere, which may occur long after excavation. These time-dependent deformation processes must be considered in the assessment of tunnel stability and excavation behaviour. Given the specialized nature of swelling phenomena and their sensitivity to mineralogy and exposure duration, their stability assessment should be addressed in detail during the detailed design stage. However, for preliminary tunnel stability and excavation behaviour evaluations, and due to comparable SRF classification ranges, swelling rock conditions are treated similarly to squeezing ground pressure zones.

Heavy Spalling and Rock Bursting Zone

Support must be installed immediately to prevent progressive failure. High-energy-absorbing or dynamic rock bolts should be utilised, together with shotcrete linings reinforced by energy-absorbing steel wire mesh or equivalent systems capable of accommodating sudden rock detachment and dynamic loads.
